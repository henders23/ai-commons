## What you get

A position paper of around 4,000 words that makes a field-level argument in eight short sections.

- **The claim.** Academic literacies should provide the central intellectual framework for engagement with generative AI in academic learning and communication, and EAP practitioners should help lead its development with disciplinary colleagues, students and other specialists.
- **A working definition** of generative academic literacies, with three distinctions kept precise: academic literacies as the theoretical orientation, EAP as the field that can organise and deliver the work, and GAL as a proposed programme at their intersection.
- **What would justify a subfield**, and why the paper declines to claim one yet.
- **The case for the programme**, and why in-sessional EAP is a promising base where it has established curriculum relationships.
- **Objections that should shape the design**, taken seriously rather than dismissed.
- **What GAL would build.** An openly accessible commons of teaching resources and evidence, a four-stage practitioner-development pathway, a research programme, and an earned institutional advisory role.
- **What would count as success, revision or failure**, with proposed indicators and a review timetable.

## How to use it

**On your own.** This is the paper that explains why this Commons exists and what it is trying to become. Section 6.1 describes the Commons as the programme's shared infrastructure, and sets a standard for what every resource here should specify.

**With your team.** Section 5, the objections, is the productive place to start a discussion: ask which objections your own centre would raise and whether the design answers them. Section 7 gives a team a way to agree in advance what evidence would change its mind.

**For a centre position.** Section 6.4 describes how EAP earns an advisory role on assessment and AI policy rather than claiming one. That framing is useful when a centre is deciding what to ask of its institution.

## Argument in brief

Generative AI raises a familiar educational problem in a changed form: how do students learn to make, evaluate and take responsibility for knowledge through academic communication? Academic literacies connects texts with the people, practices and institutions that give them meaning, and EAP brings expertise in discourse, disciplinary communication and teaching. The immediate commitment is to better learning, more defensible assessment and more equitable participation. A name, a repository and institutional recognition are means to those ends, and the proposal should be judged by what changes for students and educators, including when the best decision is to limit or decline AI use.
