## What you get

A position paper of around 9,000 words, written from an in-sessional academic literacies perspective. It draws on three bodies of evidence: theories of authorship and writer identity, the redefinition of authorship in scholarly publishing and integrity policy, and assessment research.

- **A definition of authorship for assessment purposes.** The student's demonstrable capacity to take responsibility for, explain and defend the meaning of their work, including any use of AI, within the conventions of their discipline.
- **Eight principles for teachers and course designers.** Design for authorship rather than detect it; make accountability the standard; adopt a two-lane, programmatic design; specify permitted use per task and teach disclosure; assess process and judgement; teach source use and voice explicitly; lead with equity; embed rather than bolt on.
- **Worked examples across nine disciplines**, from the laboratory report to the design portfolio, showing what accountable authorship looks like in each.
- **The EAP precedent.** A section on how EAP has faced this question before, in the debates over proofreading and permissible help, and what that history teaches.
- **A section on what would change the position**, so the argument can be tested rather than simply accepted.
- Full references, and an appendix on sources and verification.

## How to use it

**On your own.** Read it through once. Then reread section 8, the definition and principles, and ask which of the eight your own practice already meets.

**With your team.** The paper is written to be argued with. Sections 5.3 on detection and 6.1 on the two-lane model are the most useful starting points for a discussion, and section 8 gives a team eight concrete things to agree or disagree about. Allow 60 to 90 minutes.

**For a centre position.** The definition in section 8.1 is offered as a draft that a centre can adopt, adapt or reject. The principles map onto the sections a position statement usually needs.

## Argument in brief

Generative AI has reopened an old question, what is an author, inside the everyday machinery of university assessment. Scholarly publishing answered it quickly and almost unanimously: an author is someone who can be held accountable for the work, not someone who typed the words. The paper argues that student assessment can adopt the same standard with confidence, that AI-text detection is both technically unsound and discriminatory against multilingual writers, and that in-sessional academic literacies provision, which already teaches source use, voice and reflection, is well placed to help disciplines put the principles into practice.
